Eve's Eve

The brass key is warm in your fist, and you have walked far enough to stop pretending this is errand work. The house on the hill waits through the storm, its windows dark except one, and you are certain that when you turn the key in the lock, everything will end: the door will give, you will step inside, close it, keep it shut, and walk away from the last eleven years. No one is coming with you. No one will see you do it. That is the only ending you have allowed yourself, so you repeat it under your breath.

Halfway along the lane the water comes down in sheets, and you walk faster, shoulders hunched. You know this road from every version of the story you told yourself: the lane, the hedge, the gate that never latched, the house at the end of everything, perched on the hill like a threat. The storm has been moving inland all afternoon, and now it is on top of you, the sky a low grey ceiling, the world a few metres of tunnel. You count your steps the way you used to, as a child, to keep from crying. You are not crying. You are counting.

Under the eaves of the porch the wind changes its voice, and you stand there a moment before you knock. You have the key, but you knock anyway, three taps, the pattern from childhood, the one your mother taught you as a test of patience. The house answers with the creak of old wood. Then you see it: a light in the kitchen window, low and amber, steady in the dark. Your mother, Eve, has been dead a year and a day, and the kettle has no business boiling, and the light has no business burning, and you do not think about it, because thinking is a luxury you have spent.

No one is inside, or so the lawyer's letter said: the house is empty, the contents sold, the estate wound down, and you are expected to appear and sign. You have read the letter so many times the words have gone soft. Empty. Sold. Wound down. The house, you have been told, is a box with a past. You take the handle, and it is cold under your palm, and the door is open, it has been open the whole time, standing ajar. You do not open it. No lock gives. You have not needed a key all evening, and it takes you a moment to realize the certainty you carried up the lane was never about the lock.

Darkness takes the moment you step inside, and then your phone's screen finds you, and the house is smaller than memory, narrower, the wallpaper the colour of weak tea. The hallway smells of dust, and something older underneath, something like the inside of a stopped clock. Through the glass the light in the kitchen is still on, steady as a held breath. You set your phone down on the hall table, because you always put things back, the one habit your mother never managed to break in you, and you stand with your hand flat on the table's edge, feeling the wood take your warmth and give it back.

Every room is one you have not allowed yourself to enter, and you move through them in order. The study, where the letter says the only thing left is a filing cabinet. The guest room, its curtains still drawn. And the kitchen, where you go to look, because the light, because the kettle: a lamp still burning, a kettle still warm, a house that is not empty, not really, and you stand in the doorway with that warmth on your face like weather, and you do not breathe, because a person in a warm kitchen has to explain the kettle. In your fist the brass key sits against your palm, and it is warm, warmer than it has any right to be. You go to the study.

Room by room, the house gives you its contents. The study is a room of paper: the calendar from last year on the wall, and on the desk, a tape recorder you had not known your mother kept, the old box model, and beside it, a card in her handwriting: For when the weather turns. You sit in her chair. The chair takes the shape of you without any effort, as if it has been waiting all year to be right for you, and outside the storm is at its loudest, the house swaying, and you do not press the button. Not yet. The two words are not on the card. They are in the air, and they have been in the air all evening, and you have been walking past them.

Slowly you press the button, and your mother's voice comes out of the little speaker, thin and clear, as if from the other room. "You don't have to come in. I just needed you to know the light was on." That is all. The tape clicks, and the silence that follows has a shape. You sit, hands flat on the chair, and you understand it was not sent to a visitor. It was sent to a daughter who was never expected to arrive, a daughter who would stand on the porch with the key in her fist and the light in the kitchen and be exactly as certain, now, that something was wrong. You are that daughter. You have always been that daughter. The storm hammers the windows, and you let it.

The tape is not finished. The next recording begins, a pause, and then a voice you have heard in the bathroom mirror for thirty years: "If this is playing, you are already home. The door is open. Go back the way you came, and let it close behind you." That is your voice. It is calm in a way you have not been all evening, and it calls the house you just walked into home, and you sit with the recorder ticking, and you try to remember this morning, properly, and you find it the way you find the bottom of a pool at low water. You were here. This morning. The lamp on. The kettle on. The house empty except for you, and then not you, and then the storm.

One by one, the rooms settle their meaning, the way a hand prints into wet sand. The hall table where you set the phone: it is not there. It is on the kitchen counter, the screen dark, and you remember setting it down this morning. The study chair that knew your shape: a coat draped over the back of it, and you know that coat, you are wearing that coat, and it is there, over the chair, and you do not look again. And the calendar on the wall. Today's date. And in the margin of the date, in your own handwriting: not yet. Not yet. Not yet, as if you were promising the day you would not do this. As if you were promising the day you would.

Really, you are not afraid of the house. That is the thing the storm tried all evening to make you admit, and it is true: you have been afraid of everything else, the house never afraid of you, which is why you left it, which is why the key, which is why you have been walking in a circle all evening and calling it a road. The storm is not coming in here. The storm is the one thing you brought with you. The weather was never the point. It was the shape you needed the evening to have, the shape of an ending you could walk through and survive. And then, in the silence, you say it: "I'm leaving, then." The words hang in the storm-dark. The house has heard them. It does not argue, and that is the most terrifying thing.

Mostly, now, you are walking. You walk to the front door with the brass key in your fist and stand on the threshold, and you finally see the lock the way it is. The key is cut for the inside. It never was not. From the outside it can only ever be a key for closing: the door was never the thing you opened, it was the thing you were always closing, and you carried its only use all evening. You step back inside, you turn the key, the bolt throws, the house locks itself, and you step out into the storm, and the door closes behind you and stays shut, and the house goes dark, one window at a time. And now you understand what the first moment was never saying. You were never coming in. The walk you have just walked is the long way out, the lane the whole evening insisted was uphill: this is how a leaving lets itself be told, as a coming, walked in reverse, the only direction a memory of leaving can travel once it decides to be lived again. The kitchen light will stay on for a while, because you left it on, and you always do. The door is shut. The key is in your hand. You are the one who was always leaving, and the storm is yours, and it follows you down the hill, and it does not let go.
