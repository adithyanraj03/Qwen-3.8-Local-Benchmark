Never Odd or Even

The brass key is in your hand, and it has the cold of the step it lies on, and the yellow house on the hill takes you in as if it has been doing nothing else for eleven winters but waiting for this exact minute. The door shuts behind you with a small, satisfied sound, and the hall is warm, and a lamp burns in the corner of the parlor, and past the glass the storm is already at work on the fields, filling the night with the sound of the world being rearranged. You do not turn around. You have never been good at turning around, and the storm goes on working while the house holds you, and the house has been good at holding things.

Half the hall is lamp-light and the other half is the dark of the night, and you stand in the middle of the old floor, where the boards know your weight, and the storm presses its face to the windows on the far wall, and you hear it the way you hear yourself, not as a sound but as a fact. She is past the parlor door, moving slowly, setting out cups, and the clock on the mantel is counting the night in even, patient ticks, and the warmth of the place settles over you like a coat you have worn before, and you stand still, because standing still is the only thing you have ever been asked to do.

Under the lamp she turns, for one moment, and you see her the way the storm sees everything, all at once, all the way down: the grey in her hair, the coat she has not taken off, the way she sets the second cup down and lets the steam go where it will. "You're early this year," she says, and the clock in the hall agrees with her, and you know, the way you always know, that it is not early, that it has been waiting the whole year, that the winter has been walking toward this minute since the first frost, when the house began its slow work of keeping one light burning.

No one teaches you what to do with eleven winters. You know the table is set for two and that the second place is yours; you know the storm has walked this road since the first frost; you know the words you are going to say to her, the ones you have said a hundred times in the hall of your own mind, where the door is always open, and the lamp is always burning, and the second cup is always steaming, and the weather is always outside the glass, waiting to be let in. You know this year is different, though you cannot say what, only that the night is longer, and your hand is full, the way it has always been full in winters.

Dinner would be on the table if she had made it, but she has only made the cups, and that is enough, because you are not here for the table, not exactly, you are here for the keeping of the light, and the waiting is the weather's oldest form, and the storm outside is doing the waiting with you, matching your silence the way a mirror matches a face, and neither of you says anything, and the night goes on, and the house stands in the dark like a held breath, and you stand in the middle of the house, and the waiting goes on until the clock changes its mind.

Even so you cross the hall. Your fingers find the brass key again and you try the lock the way a man tries a lock, out of habit, not to open it but to make sure it is there, and the metal refuses you the way it has refused you for eleven winters, cold and round and absolute. "It doesn't fit anymore," she says, and you know she is not speaking to the door, and you do not tell her what it has always fit, which is nothing, and you keep the metal in your hand the way you keep everything, by not letting go, and the lamp goes on burning, and the storm goes on pressing, and the night holds both of them the way a wall holds a cold wind.

Remember what the town says, and what the town does not say. The town says the sea took him, and the town does not say which sea, or whose, or why the waves stood up that evening like a held breath. The town says the search went on for a week and found nothing but the boat, and the town does not say that the storm never left the parish, that it has been standing on this porch ever since, hand to the door, waiting for a door it was never going to open. The town does a lot of not saying, and you have always been good at keeping other people's silences, and in the parlor the lamp holds its one bright note, and in the dark your hand holds its one cold weight.

She comes out of the parlor while the lamp holds its one bright note against the night, and she stands where the light can find her, and she looks at you the way you look at the house, all at once and all the way down. The storm is at the far window now, close enough to bow the glass, and the glass bows, and she does not flinch, and you want to tell her that you are sorry, and the words go out of you into the night and land on the fields, and are carried down to the sea, and she watches you the way she has watched you for eleven winters, with the door shut and the one light still burning.

The lamp goes out at the second hour, or it seems to, and the dark inside the house is the dark of a held room, the dark of a place that has stopped expecting anything, and the dark outside the glass is the dark of a moving one, the dark of a place that is expecting everything, and you stand between them, because you have always stood between them, with your back to the window and the storm pressing closer, and she is still standing in the parlor doorway, and the silence between you is the only weather the house has ever known, and it is very loud, and the night arrives, and stands, and does not move.

Once there was a man with a coat, and a boat in the cove, and a word left in the air before the light was gone, and the sea kept him anyway, and the woman stopped waiting for the door and started waiting for the weather instead, and the house has not stopped holding the shape of him, the way a fist holds the shape of a stone it has thrown. You know this. You have always known this, the way the sea knows the cove. Knowing is the only room you have ever lived in, and the house, holding its shape for eleven winters, is not a house so much as a held shape, and the night stands very still, because the minute you have been walking toward all year is almost here.

Right now the door is the loudest thing in the world, and you are facing it, and your hand is flat on the wood, and the wood does not give, and the woman has her back to the window now, and you are pressing your face to the house the way the house presses its face to the sea, and she says, "The door is locked anyway," and her voice comes to you the way weather comes to a window, from very far away, and her voice is not cold, and her voice is not kind, and her voice is the weather's oldest form, and you press harder, and the wood does not give, and the storm presses with you, and the minute is almost here.

Morning: the storm leaves the hill at first light, the way it has left it every year for eleven winters, and the house sits dry and quiet, and on the step where it lies every winter, the brass key is cold, and the woman comes out and does not pick it up, because she knows what it is. And you know now what the first minute was, the minute the yellow house took you in and the door shut with its small, satisfied sound: it never shut, and it never opened, and you never crossed the threshold, and the hall was never yours, and you are not the man with the coat, you are the weather that took the man with the coat, and the house is not taking you in, the house is remembering him through the wood, and the key is not a key at all, it is the sign she leaves out every winter so that you will come, and try, and go, and the door stays shut, and the door has always been shut, and you go out over the fields and down to the sea, and do not stop, and do not stop.
