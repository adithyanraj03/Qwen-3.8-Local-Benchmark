Live not on evil

The wiper blades count the seconds, and you let them. Twenty years of rehearsal have brought you to this driveway, to this sheet of water hammering the roof of the car, to this small brass key riding in your coat pocket like a tooth pulled out long ago. You will not knock first. You will wait for the crack of the door, the way you waited for it as a boy, press the key into the gap, say the sentence you have been polishing since you were seventeen, and drive until the house is a wet black dot and the dot is nothing. He wanted the last word. Fine. You will give it to him folded around a piece of brass.

He never shouted, which was the whole problem. He lowered his voice the way other men lower a drawbridge, and you — seventeen, all ladders and matches — had spent the evening burning bridges back. The truck, the girl, the summer you swore you would fix: he listened with his hands flat on the table, and when you ran out of matches he said the only thing he needed to say, quietly, from behind the drawbridge: "Don't come back until you mean it." You meant to slam the door so hard the whole house would remember your name. Instead you went out into the storm that had been gathering over the lake all afternoon, and the water took you before the door could take you back. You never got the chance. You were too busy leaving.

Until the storm finished what it had started, you slept in the truck with the windows cracked, and by morning you had a plan, and a plan at seventeen is a law. You would come home on one condition: that he admit he had locked you out. Not the door — you. You would not set foot under that roof until he said the words. You drove back in the gray light, saw the white door shut and, you were so sure, bolted and barred against you. You circled the block once. You did not stop. Laws are kept at a distance.

Nineteen winters is a long time to keep a law. You called at holidays in the beginning, then at one holiday, then you let the years file past. You built a life the way you build everything: fast, and with an exit. And still, twice a year, you drove the eight miles past the house and did not turn in: August, when the lake wind comes off the water; March, when the storms come in low off the ridge, the kind that bend the spruce flat. You learned that house the way you learn a weather map — from the road, by its moods. The porch light off. The porch light on. The porch light on.

Dad died on a Wednesday, which is the wrong day for anything. Marta called you at work, and you heard her say the word died, and all you could think about was the meeting you were going to miss. That is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it arrives late, and it arrives in the wrong currency. She said come home. You said next week. Next week became the month, the month became the season, and you kept that phone call in your pocket the way you kept the other thing — worn smooth, heavy as a coin you could not spend.

Even at the funeral you carried the brass key in your overcoat, two pockets down, a small hard planet in its orbit. You had palmed it off the hook by the door that night — you understood that only later, in the back row while they let it down: you had taken the only thing that opened that house, and kept it ever since, and you told yourself it was spite. It was not spite. Spite is light; it floats. This was ballast. You did not cry. You made a plan, the way you make all your plans: cold, and at a distance. Marta would have changed the locks by now; everyone changes the locks. You would drive out on the first bad weather, hand the thing through the crack of the door, and let the whole business end.

Route 9 was under water by the county line, and you drove through it like a man paying a debt in installments. The radio said the river had come out of its banks, said the old bridge was closed, said stay home, and you kept going, because the storm was the excuse you had been waiting for all year. You could not come on a clear day; a clear day would mean you wanted something. You could only come when the weather made it necessary. You almost turned around at the flooded bridge. You did not turn around. You have never once turned around, and you know it, and knowing it is how you keep from knowing the rest.

Standing in the driveway with the engine ticking over, you take in the house the way you took it in from the road for nineteen years: all at once, and from a distance. The siding is the same gray. The spruce is the same spruce, bent now, older, still bent. The barn roof is patched with new metal. And the porch light is on — warm, steady, patient in the storm — the way it was on in August, the way it was on in March, the way you decided, years ago, to stop noticing. You cut the engine. The water on the roof goes on drumming. You sit in it a long time. The sentence you brought is ready, the way a stone is ready.

Twenty years compress to the length of a walk — six steps from the car to the porch — and you take them in the storm, and by the third step your hair is down your neck and by the fifth you are seventeen again: soaked, furious, invincible, a boy with a law to keep. The porch boards remember your weight. The screen door hangs a little crooked, the way it always hung, the way he always said, not yet, not this week, next week. And there is the door behind it, the white door with the paint scratched away by the handle where you used to drop things, and you reach into your pocket for the proof of everything, and your hand is shaking, and you hate that your hand is shaking.

One hand on the door and one hand in your pocket, you wait for the bolt. You wait for the click of it — the deadbolt, the locked door, the last word. You knock: three times, the old knock, the one that meant I'm here, I'm hungry, let me in. You stand in the water with your knuckles still on the panel, and inside you hear a clock, and footsteps, and your own name said in a voice you have not heard in twenty years — said the way it was said in this house when you were small and something had broken and the one who broke it was calling for help before the yelling started.

Right as the latch lifts you step back, because you have a plan, and plans are kept at a distance, and you have the sentence, and the sentence is the only reason you are standing in a flooded driveway in the storm. The door swings inward — all the way inward, unhurried, the way a door swings when nothing holds it back — and Marta is there in her house coat, hair wet, older, gray at the temples, exactly your father's face on a woman's shoulders. She looks at your hands: at the thing in your right hand, held out like a coin, like a tooth, like a stone. She starts to laugh. Then she stops, and she says: "He never changed the locks."

Marta steps aside, and you come in, and the hallway is the hallway, and she makes you tea with her back to you, and while the kettle waits she tells you the rest. He kept the porch light on every night the weather turned, because you were always coming home in a storm. He would not let her change the lock — and when she asked him why, she says, he used to point at the hook by the door where the brass key used to hang, the one you took the night you left, and he used to say: "He'll come back when the weather turns. He'll need his key." And you, sitting at your father's table with the tea going cold in front of you and the sentence you polished for twenty years still in your mouth — useless now, a stone with nothing left to build — you understand at last what you carried all those years. Not proof that you were locked out. Proof that there was always a way back in. He never wanted the last word. He wanted the door. You set the brass key on the nail beside the hook where it belongs, and you let the storm finish, and you stay.
