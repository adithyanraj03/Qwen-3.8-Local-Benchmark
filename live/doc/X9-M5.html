Drawn onward

There was one rule in my grandmother's house, and she wore it on a hook at her belt: while the sky is at war, we do not open the door. She said it the way other people say grace, without ceremony, and then she would touch the small brass key that hung beside her house key, warm from her hip, gone soft with handling until the teeth of it had rounded like a worn coin. I was ten the summer she said it, and I took rules for a kind of weather, something that happened to you rather than for you. When she died that August, the doctor said her heart and I said nothing, and my mother put the key in my pocket without looking at me, the way you hand over a debt. That night the glass began to rattle in the sashes, and I did not understand the rule, and that was the whole of my grief, that I believed a dead woman could still be asked.

Hush first, then hurry: the storm came up off the fields the way a rumour comes up in a house. The air went the colour of a bruise's edge, and every leaf in the yard turned its pale belly up in salute to something I could not see arriving. Then the wind leaned its whole weight on the rafters and the old house groaned like a ship with a grudge. I stood in the hall with the small warm weight of it in my hand and counted the seconds between the light and the crack, and the count came down four, three, two, and at one the sky split so close that the dark after it had texture, and I understood that the house was the only dry thing left in the world, and that it was not enough.

Understanding came late to me, as it does. That night I was alone in it, and a child alone in a house at night will make a religion of any noise. My grandmother lay past the parlour with the door standing open for formality, the way the neighbours would want, and I did not go in to her, I could not have told you then why. The boards ticked as they cooled. The pipes knocked like someone patient. And beneath all of that, under the storm's boot on the roof, I heard the floodwater come up the lane and lay its flat hand on the front door and feel along the edges of it, testing the fit of things, the way a borrower tests a lock he means to break.

Not long after that came the knock. Three raps, small and even, with no hurry in them at all, the knock of someone certain of an answer. I crossed the hall with the small brass thing so tight in my fist that its ring left a red circle in my palm, and I put my ear to the paint, and I heard a child's breathing on the other side of the boards, close, the quick warm whistle of somebody who has been running and is not yet cold. "Please let me in before it finishes." I was ten, and I had been told all my life that a child does not go out alone in weather like this, and so I reasoned, the way children reason, that a child out alone in weather like this must be saved.

Determined as any child is, I drew the bolt. The door took the wind out of my hands and swung it wide, and the storm came in over the threshold like an animal into a barn, and there on the step stood a boy about my age, soaked to the colour of tallow, his hair flat on his skull, his mouth working at a word he could not get the breath for. Lightning came and went, and in the afterimage of it he was still there, and that is how I knew he was real, because lightning lies about everything else. I reached down and took his hand. It was warm. I remember that more than anything else from that night, that his hand was warm and mine was cold, and I thought nothing of it, the way you think nothing of the ground under you.

Everything about him pulled. He would not say his name, and he would not say where his mother had gone; he only pulled at my hand toward the dark beyond the porch, and I went, God help me, I went, because the storm had made a hole in the world where the lane used to be and he was the only direction left in it. I looked back once at the open door, at the warm square of hall light with my grandmother's brass key lying on the floor where I had dropped it, patient as a tooth. Then the boy pulled, and the dark closed behind me like water over a stone, and the door of the house slammed with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence nobody had finished saying.

Ravished of lane and bridge and bank, the world after that is no country, only water. The lane went first, then the difference between swimming and walking. The storm had taken the fields and the fences and the names of things, and what was left ran black and fast and without opinion, and it took us the way a road takes a coin. I could not have told you how long we went, or whether I was breathing. The boy's hand never once went cold in mine. We climbed through the dark the way you climb through a dream of your own house, certain of every turn, and I kept thinking only: his mother will be at the far side of this, and then we will be three, and then it will be a story.

Silence fell the way a lid falls. The storm stopped all at once, mid-sentence, and the quiet that filled the world afterward had weight to it, and pressure, the held breath of something enormous leaning close to look at us. The boy turned to me in that quiet, and his face was my face, ten years old, red-eyed, furious with being alive, and he smiled at me with my own mouth, and I felt the cold arrive at last, up through my feet, unhurried, like a tide that has all the time there is.

Then the boy spoke, and his voice was the voice from the other side of the door. "I have been on the step for forty years, and you were never the one who opened it." Then he let go of my hand, and he went down into the black water without a splash, and I stood there holding nothing, and the nothing was colder than the water had ever been. I walked home alone through a world that no longer resisted my feet at all, and I did not look back, because I had begun, without knowing it, to understand.

Open to the lane, the house stood lit and dry at its end, every window a gold square, and in the parlour I could see them through the glass: my grandmother asleep in her chair with her hands folded in her lap over something small and gone warm, and behind her chair a woman of forty with wet hair and a mouth that did not move, one hand resting on the old woman's shoulder the way you rest a hand on a sleeping animal, or a grave. The woman in the glass lifted her face as if she had felt me looking, and I saw that it was the face I had been wearing for thirty years, and the face I would wear, and the face wearing me.

Reread now, the first paragraph of my life was a door I had already opened, and would open, and was opening now, forever, in a house with no clocks in it. The boy on the step had not been asking to come in; he had been asking to be let out. And the small brass thing at her belt was never for the door at all; it was for me, the way a bit is for a horse, the way a rule is for a child, the way a mercy is for the thing that keeps coming back, and my grandmother had known, the way you know your own name in the dark, that the storm does not knock, the storm borrows, and what it lends it always comes to collect with interest.

My grandmother lifted her head, looked past her own parlour fire, through the glass and the dark and the years, straight at me, and said the only words she ever said to me. "Not yet, love." So I sit down on the step among the wet, to wait for the storm to end, and it never ends, and I will open the door anyway, because I always have, because I always will, and in the morning a woman will wake with the taste of flood in her mouth and a small brass key warm in her hand, and she will tell a child about a rule, and I will not understand it, and I will be so glad to hear her say it.
