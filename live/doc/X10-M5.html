Four Proofs

Written so that a strong undergraduate can follow every step. Every invocation of a lemma, theorem, or arithmetic law is named. The companion script check.py verifies each statement numerically for small cases.

0. Conventions and preliminaries

Conventions. $\mathbb{Z}$ denotes the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ the rationals, $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ the positive integers. "$a$ divides $b$", written $a \mid b$, means: there exists an integer $k$ with $b = ak$. A prime is an integer $p \ge 2$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$. We write $a \equiv b \pmod m$ to mean $m \mid (a-b)$.

We collect the few external facts the proofs need.

Lemma 0.1 (Well-ordering principle). Every non-empty set of positive integers has a least element. Use: standard axiom of the integers (equivalent to induction); we cite it in the proof of Lemma 0.2.

Lemma 0.2 (Bézout's identity). For integers $a, b$, not both zero, there exist integers $x, y$ with $\gcd(a,b) = ax + by$. Proof. Let $S = \{ax + by : x, y \in \mathbb{Z}\} \cap \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$. The set $S$ is non-empty: if $a \ne 0$, then $a\cdot 1 + b \cdot 0 = a \in S$ when $a > 0$, and $a \cdot(-1) = -a \in S$ when $a<0$; if $a = 0$ then $b \ne 0$ and the same argument applies to $b$. By Lemma 0.1, $S$ has a least element; call it $d = ax_0 + by_0$.

We claim $d \mid a$. By the division algorithm, write $a = qd + r$ with integers $q, r$ and $0 \le r < d$. Then $$ r = a - qd = a - q(ax_0 + by_0) = a(1 - qx_0) + b(-qy_0), $$ so $r$ is an integer combination of $a$ and $b$. If $r > 0$, then $r \in S$ and $r < d$, contradicting the minimality of $d$. Hence $r = 0$, i.e. $d \mid a$. The same argument with the roles of $a$ and $b$ swapped gives $d \mid b$, so $d$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$.

Finally, any common divisor $c$ of $a$ and $b$ divides every integer combination of $a$ and $b$, in particular $d = ax_0 + by_0$; so $c \le d$. Thus $d$ is the greatest common divisor: $d = \gcd(a,b)$. $\square$

Lemma 0.3 (Euclid's lemma). If $p$ is prime and $p \mid ab$, then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$. Proof. Suppose $p \nmid a$. Then $\gcd(p, a) = 1$: indeed any common divisor of $p$ and $a$ divides $p$, so it is $1$ or $p$, and it is not $p$ because $p \nmid a$. By Lemma 0.2 there exist integers $x, y$ with $1 = px + ay$. Multiply by $b$: $$ b = bpx + aby. $$ Now $p \mid bpx$ (as $p$ is a factor) and $p \mid aby$ (since $p \mid ab$ by hypothesis, and divisors divide multiples). A divisor of two integers divides their sum, so $p \mid b$. We have shown: if $p \nmid a$ then $p \mid b$, which is the claim. $\square$

Lemma 0.4 (Multiplicativity of congruences). If $a \equiv a' \pmod m$ and $b \equiv b' \pmod m$, then $ab \equiv a'b' \pmod m$. Proof. By hypothesis, $a - a' = mr$ and $b - b' = ms$ for some integers $r, s$. Then $$ ab - a'b' = ab - a'b + a'b - a'b' = (a-a')b + a'(b-b') = mr b + a' m s = m(rb + a's), $$ so $m \mid (ab - a'b')$, i.e. $ab \equiv a' b' \pmod m$. $\square$

Corollary 0.5. Any product of integers each congruent to $1 \bmod 4$ is itself congruent to $1 \bmod 4$. Proof. By Lemma 0.4, if $u \equiv 1$ and $v \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ then $uv \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. An easy induction on the number of factors (pairing off one factor at a time) extends this to any finite product. $\square$

Lemma 0.6 (Existence of prime factors). Every integer $N \ge 2$ has a prime divisor. Proof. Suppose some integer $\ge 2$ has no prime divisor, and let $T$ be the set of these; by Lemma 0.1, $T$ has a least element $t$. Since $t$ has no prime divisor, $t$ is not prime, so $t = uv$ with integers $u, v$ satisfying $2 \le u < t$ and $2 \le v < t$. Neither $u$ nor $v$ lies in $T$ (minimality of $t$), so $u$ has a prime divisor $q$; then $q \mid u \mid t$, so $q \mid t$, contradicting that $t$ has no prime divisor. Hence $T$ is empty. $\square$

1. Infinitely many primes $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$

Theorem 1.1. There are infinitely many primes $p$ with $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

Proof. Suppose, for a contradiction, that there are only finitely many such primes. List them as $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k$ (the list is non-empty, since $3$ is prime and $3 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$). Define $$ N \;=\; 4\,p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k \;-\; 1. $$

Step 1: $N \ge 2$. Since $p_1 = 3$ is among the $p_i$, the product $p_1 \cdots p_k \ge 3$, so $N \ge 4 \cdot 3 - 1 = 11 \ge 2$.

Step 2: $N \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. Each $p_i$ divides $P := p_1 \cdots p_k$, so $4P \equiv 0 \pmod 4$, hence $N = 4P - 1 \equiv -1 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

Step 3: $N$ has a prime divisor $q$ with $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. By Step 1 and Lemma 0.6, $N$ has a prime divisor $q$; write $N = q \cdot M$ with $M \ge 1$. Since $N$ is odd (an even number is $\equiv 0$ or $2 \pmod 4$, but $N \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ by Step 2), $q$ is odd. By the division algorithm every odd prime is $\equiv 1$ or $3 \pmod 4$. Suppose, for contradiction, that every prime divisor of $N$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Then $N$, being a product of primes each $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (namely the prime factorization of $N$, which exists by iterating Lemma 0.6), would be $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ by Corollary 0.5, contradicting Step 2. Hence some prime divisor $q$ of $N$ satisfies $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

Step 4: $q$ is not on the list. If $q = p_i$ for some $i$, then $q \mid P$, so $q \mid 4P$. Together with $q \mid N$ this gives $$ q \;\mid\; \big(4P - N\big) \;=\; 1, $$ because a common divisor of two integers divides their difference. But no prime divides $1$ (a prime is $\ge 2$), a contradiction. So $q \ne p_i$ for every $i$.

Conclusion. Step 3 produces a prime $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and Step 4 shows it is not on the complete list $p_1, \dots, p_k$ — a contradiction. Therefore the list cannot be finite, and there are infinitely many primes $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$. $\square$

Remark. The same argument with $N = 4P+1$ fails: a number $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ can have all prime factors $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$ (e.g. $9 = 3 \cdot 3$), which is exactly why we used the minus sign.

2. Irrationality of $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$

We first need a small lemma.

Lemma 2.1. $\sqrt{6}$ is irrational.

Proof. Suppose $\sqrt 6 = a/b$ with integers $a, b$, $b \ne 0$, and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ (such a representation exists: start with any representation and cancel the common divisor $\gcd(a,b)$, which is legitimate because $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{a/\gcd(a,b)}{b/\gcd(a,b)}$). Squaring gives $6b^2 = a^2$.

  • Since $a^2 = 6b^2 = 2 \cdot (3b^2)$, we have $2 \mid a^2$. As $2$ is prime, Euclid's lemma (Lemma 0.3, applied to $a^2 = a \cdot a$) gives $2 \mid a$. Write $a = 2c$.
  • Substituting: $6b^2 = (2c)^2 = 4c^2$, so $3b^2 = 2c^2$ after dividing both sides by $2$ (valid since $\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero divisors: $2x = 2y \Rightarrow x = y$). The right-hand side $2c^2$ is even, so $2 \mid 3b^2$; by Euclid's lemma, $2 \mid 3$ or $2 \mid b^2$, and $2 \nmid 3$, so $2 \mid b^2$. Applying Euclid's lemma once more to $b^2 = b \cdot b$ gives $2 \mid b$.

But then $2$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$, contradicting $\gcd(a,b) = 1$. Hence $\sqrt 6$ is irrational. $\square$

Theorem 2.2. $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ is irrational.

Proof. Suppose, for a contradiction, that $r := \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Step 1. $r > 0$: both $\sqrt 2$ and $\sqrt 3$ are positive (the square root of a positive number is defined to be the positive solution of $x^2 = n$, and $2, 3 > 0$), and the sum of positive numbers is positive. In particular $r \ne 0$.

Step 2. Square both sides of $\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3 = r$: $$ 5 + 2\sqrt{6} \;=\; 2 + 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{3} + 3 \;=\; (\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3})^2 \;=\; r^2, $$ where we used the distributive law (FOIL) and $\sqrt{2}\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{6}$, which holds because both sides are positive and their squares are equal: $(\sqrt2\sqrt3)^2 = 2 \cdot 3 = 6 = (\sqrt6)^2$.

Step 3. Solve for $\sqrt 6$: $$ \sqrt{6} \;=\; \frac{r^2 - 5}{2}. $$ Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under multiplication, subtraction and division by a non-zero number ($2 \ne 0$), the right-hand side is rational. Hence $\sqrt 6 \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Conclusion. Step 3 contradicts Lemma 2.1. Therefore $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ is irrational. $\square$

Remark (the polynomial viewpoint, which check.py tests numerically). Let $\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$. Unwinding Step 2 twice: $\alpha^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt 6$ gives $\alpha^2 - 5 = 2\sqrt 6$, and squaring again gives $(\alpha^2 - 5)^2 = 24$, i.e. $$ \alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 1 = 0, $$ so $\alpha$ is a root of $f(x) = x^4 - 10x^2 + 1 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ (exact check: $(5+2\sqrt6)^2 = 25 + 20\sqrt{6} + 24 = 49 + 20\sqrt 6 = 10(5 + 2\sqrt 6) - 1$). If $\alpha = a/b$ were rational in lowest terms, then $b^4 f(a/b) = a^4 - 10a^2b^2 + b^4 = 0$. Reducing modulo $3$ (where $10 \equiv 1$) gives $a^4 - a^2 b^2 + b^4 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$. Squares modulo $3$ are $0$ or $1$, so $a^2 \equiv A$ and $b^2 \equiv B$ with $A, B \in {0, 1}$, and the congruence reads $A^2 - AB + B^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$; checking the four cases $(A,B) \in \{0,1\}^2$, the value is $0$ only for $(A,B) = (0,0)$ (it equals $1$ in the other three cases). Hence $3 \mid a^2$ and $3 \mid b^2$, and Euclid's lemma (with the prime $3$) forces $3 \mid a$ and $3 \mid b$, contradicting lowest terms — a third route to the same conclusion. Equivalently, by Gauss's lemma (if a monic polynomial with integer coefficients has a rational root, that root is an integer dividing the constant term), any rational root of $f$ must be $\pm 1$, and $f(1) = f(-1) = -8 \ne 0$. So $\alpha$ has degree exactly $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: no polynomial of degree $1, 2$ or $3$ with integer coefficients vanishes at $\alpha$.

3. Every finite tournament has a Hamiltonian path

Definitions. A tournament on a finite vertex set $V$ is a directed graph in which every pair of distinct vertices $u, v$ is joined by exactly one directed edge, either $u \to v$ or $v \to u$ (exactly one, by definition of a tournament). A Hamiltonian path is a sequence $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_m$ listing every vertex of $V$ exactly once ($m = |V|$) with $v_i \to v_{i+1}$ for all $1 \le i < m$.

Theorem 3.1. Every finite tournament has a Hamiltonian path.

Proof. We argue by induction on $n = |V|$, proving the statement for every $n \ge 1$.

Base case ($n = 1$). The single-vertex sequence $v_1$ lists every vertex exactly once, and there are no consecutive pairs to check, so it is a Hamiltonian path.

Inductive step. Assume the claim holds for all tournaments on $n$ vertices (induction hypothesis), and let $T$ be a tournament on $n+1$ vertices. Pick any vertex $v$ and let $T' = T - v$, the tournament obtained by deleting $v$ and all edges touching it: $T'$ has $n$ vertices, and any two of its vertices still span exactly one directed edge (deletion does not alter the remaining edges). By the induction hypothesis, $T'$ has a Hamiltonian path $$ P = \big( u_1 \to u_2 \to \cdots \to u_n \big). $$ We insert $v$ into $P$.

Case 1: $v \to u_1$. Then $v \to u_1 \to u_2 \to \cdots \to u_n$ is a Hamiltonian path of $T$: it lists all $n+1$ vertices exactly once, and every required edge either is the edge $v \to u_1$ or is an edge of $P$.

Case 2: $u_n \to v$. Then $u_1 \to \cdots \to u_n \to v$ is a Hamiltonian path, by the same verification.

Case 3: neither Case 1 nor Case 2 holds. Then $\neg(v \to u_1)$ and $\neg(u_n \to v)$. Because $T$ is a tournament, for each pair exactly one direction of the edge exists, so $\neg(v \to u_1)$ forces $u_1 \to v$, and $\neg(u_n \to v)$ forces $v \to u_n$. (Note $n \ge 2$ here: if $n = 1$, then $u_1 = u_n$, and $\neg(v \to u_1)$ together with $\neg(u_n \to v)$ would leave the pair $\{v, u_1\}$ with no edge direction, violating the tournament property.) Define $$ S = \{\, i \in \{1, \dots, n-1\} : v \to u_{i+1}\,\}. $$ By the previous sentence, $v \to u_n$ holds, i.e. $v \to u_{(n-1)+1}$, so $n - 1 \in S$; thus $S$ is non-empty. By the well-ordering principle (Lemma 0.1), $S$ has a least element $j$, with $1 \le j \le n-1$; in particular $u_{j+1}$ exists. We claim $\neg(v \to u_j)$: if $j \ge 2$, then $j - 1 \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}$ but $j - 1 \notin S$ by minimality of $j$, so $\neg\big(v \to u_{(j-1)+1}\big)$, i.e. $\neg(v \to u_j)$; and if $j = 1$, then $\neg(v \to u_j)$ is $\neg(v \to u_1)$, known since Case 1 fails. The tournament property applied to the pair $\{v, u_j\}$ now forces $$ u_j \to v. $$ Now form the sequence $$ P' = \big( u_1 \to \cdots \to u_j \to v \to u_{j+1} \to \cdots \to u_n \big). $$ Verification, edge by edge: - $u_i \to u_{i+1}$ for $i < j$: these are edges of $P$. - $u_j \to v$: just established. - $v \to u_{j+1}$: true because $j \in S$. - $u_i \to u_{i+1}$ for $i \ge j+1$: edges of $P$.

$P'$ lists $u_1, \dots, u_n$ (each exactly once, as in $P$) together with $v$, so it lists all $n+1$ vertices exactly once. Hence $P'$ is a Hamiltonian path of $T$.

In all three cases $T$ has a Hamiltonian path, completing the inductive step. By the principle of mathematical induction, every finite tournament on any number $n \ge 1$ of vertices has a Hamiltonian path. $\square$

Remark. The proof is constructive: inserting one vertex at a time yields an $O(n^2)$ algorithm, which is exactly what check.py runs on every tournament with at most $6$ vertices.

4. The sum of the first $n$ odd cubes

Theorem 4.1. For every integer $n \ge 1$, $$ 1^3 + 3^3 + \cdots + (2n-1)^3 \;=\; n^2 \big(2n^2 - 1\big). $$

Proof. Induction on $n$.

Base case ($n = 1$). The left side is the single term $1^3 = 1$; the right side is $1^2 \cdot (2 \cdot 1^2 - 1) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. Equal.

Inductive step. Assume the identity holds for some $k \ge 1$: $$ \sum_{i=1}^{k} (2i-1)^3 = k^2 (2k^2 - 1). \tag{IH} $$ Then, splitting off the last term and using (IH): $$ \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} (2i-1)^3 \;=\; k^2 (2k^2 - 1) + (2k+1)^3. $$ Expand both pieces using the binomial theorem $(a+b)^3 = a^3 + 3a^2 b + 3ab^2 + b^3$ with $a = 2k$, $b = 1$: $$ (2k+1)^3 = 8k^3 + 12k^2 + 6k + 1, \qquad k^2(2k^2 - 1) = 2k^4 - k^2. $$ Adding (collecting like powers of $k$): $$ 2k^4 - k^2 + 8k^3 + 12k^2 + 6k + 1 \;=\; 2k^4 + 8k^3 + 11k^2 + 6k + 1. \tag{$*$}$$ On the other hand, the claimed formula at $n = k+1$ is $$ (k+1)^2 \big( 2(k+1)^2 - 1 \big). $$ Expand: $(k+1)^2 = k^2 + 2k + 1$ and $2(k+1)^2 - 1 = 2k^2 + 4k + 1$. Multiplying the two polynomials term by term: $$ (k^2 + 2k + 1)(2k^2 + 4k + 1) = 2k^4 + 4k^3 + k^2 + 4k^3 + 8k^2 + 2k + 2k^2 + 4k + 1, $$ which collects to $$ 2k^4 + 8k^3 + 11k^2 + 6k + 1, $$ identical to $(*)$. Hence $$ \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} (2i-1)^3 = (k+1)^2 \big( 2(k+1)^2 - 1 \big), $$ completing the inductive step. By mathematical induction, the identity holds for all $n \ge 1$. $\square$

Alternative (telescoping). Define $F(n) = n^2(2n^2 - 1) = 2n^4 - n^2$. Expanding as above (with $a = m-1$, $b = 1$ for $(m-1)^2$, or directly) gives the exact difference identity $$ F(m) - F(m-1) \;=\; (2m-1)^3 \qquad \text{for all } m \ge 1, $$ where $F(0) = 0$. Summing from $m = 1$ to $n$, the left side telescopes: $$ \sum_{m=1}^{n} \big( F(m) - F(m-1) \big) = F(n) - F(0) = F(n), $$ since consecutive terms $+F(m)$ and $-F(m)$ cancel pairwise (a straightforward induction on $n$ formalizes this). Hence $\sum_{m=1}^n (2m-1)^3 = F(n) = n^2(2n^2-1)$. $\square$
