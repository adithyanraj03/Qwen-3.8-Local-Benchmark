Live not on evil

The brass key is in your coat pocket before you have even decided to go, cold as a coin against your thigh, while the sky above the fell goes the color of a healing bruise and the wind comes down the valley in long shuddering pushes. You stand at the kitchen window counting the flashes. One, two, three — the thunder arrives late and low, dragging itself across the moor like something wounded. Someone is out at the boathouse, inside it, and the water is rising, and you are the only living soul with the key. You pull on your father's boots, you take up the storm lantern, and you go out into the weather without hurrying, because hurrying on the moor only gets a man killed.

Hurrying or not, your mother is at the door before you reach the gate, her shawl pulled over her head like a hood, and she does not shout — she never shouts, which is worse than shouting: "You'll catch your death out there." You say there is work that cannot wait for the weather to think better of itself. She looks at you the way she has looked at you for thirty-one years, with a hope she keeps trimmed short so it cannot grow too tall, then steps back inside and closes the door on tea and smoke and leaves you to the dark. The note she left under the clock that morning is still there: bread bought, vet called, do not wait up. As if anyone ever waits up. As if anyone ever stops.

Up the track behind the yard the grass lies flat and silver under the clouds, and the gorse whips at your coat as if the whole hedge were trying to hold you back. You know this moor the way you know your own hands: where the path is firm, where it turns to peat, where the ground gives a warning and then a lesson. The wind has a new voice now, a whistle through the teeth, and the flashes come so close together that the dark never gets to settle between them. In one white instant the whole valley is a photograph: the beck swollen and braided, the farm roofs hunched, and far below the boathouse, its doors shut, its window black. You count again. One. The thunder is on top of you before you reach two.

Nobody else is out on the moor tonight; nobody sane ever is. The beck, which in August is a thread you can step across, is now a brown muscle sliding under the bridge, carrying twigs and once a whole fern, waving like a hand. You and Eli used to swim there, in the long yellow evenings when the farm was done and the world seemed invented for the two of you alone. He could hold his breath longer than any boy in the valley, he kept a knife in his boot and matches in his hat, and he swore he could hear the fish talking under the bank. You remember it so clearly that for a moment you are standing in warm shallows, and then the wind puts you back where you belong.

Down in the valley the weather changes its mind about you. The wind turns a corner of the fell and comes straight at your chest, and the first of the water arrives — hard pellets that sting the cheeks and run in rivers off the nose. The lantern gutters, shields itself, and dies, and you walk the last hundred yards by lightning alone. You go down on one knee where the path turns to slick clay, and the ground takes the lantern and keeps it, and you rise with your hands torn, your coat soaked through, and something in your chest that is not the cold. The boathouse is close now. You can hear the lake working at the piles the way a dog works at a fence.

Even in that dark, with no lantern and no stars, your hand finds the brass key in your pocket and closes on it, and the knowing of it steadies you more than the light ever did. It was Eli's key — he kept the boathouse locked against tramps and boys, and there was only ever the one, and it has been in your pocket for thirty-one years, worn smooth as a river stone. And then, out of the wind, from the direction of the shut doors, you hear him: "Throw us the key, then, quick!" His voice. Not a memory of it — the voice itself, cracked and laughing, the way he shouted everything worth shouting. Your hand shakes so badly that you have to kneel and press your palm flat into the mud to master it.

Right against the doors the storm goes quiet, the way a crowd goes quiet, and you can hear the lake heaving under the floorboards, lifting, testing. The hasp is old iron, and the hasp is shut, and you stand in black water to your ankles with your whole life narrowed down to a slot in a lock. Whoever is in there has been in there since the light went, maybe longer. You think of the gable window, how it sticks in wet weather, how it never opens the way it should. You think: if the water is over the sills in there, then whatever you are about to do, you are already too late — and you do it anyway, because that is what you have always done.

Somewhere in the valley a bell has begun to ring — roofing iron, maybe, but it keeps time like a bell, and you work the lock by feel while the water climbs your boots. Ninety-eight, the year the river took nine of the ewes, and your father said water does not negotiate; it only arrives. The bolt is stiff. The bolt has rust in its belly. Around you the night is one long grey roar, the building groans like a ship, and the lake slaps the doors now, higher than the bottom plank, so that whatever waits inside waits with its feet in the water.

The thunder stops pretending to be far away. It goes off directly overhead, a crack like a hillside breaking, and the whole sky turns white and stays white, and in that white you see the doors, the hasp, your own white hand — and the lock gives. It gives on the third try with a shriek of old iron that the storm swallows at once, and the doors swing inward on their swollen hinges, and the lake pours out through the doorway to meet you, cold to the bone, carrying weed and a wasp's drowned paper city. You lean into the frame, lanternless, blind, and call the name you have carried for thirty-one years, and the building answers with the sound of nothing moving.

Old photographs live on the mantel at home in their tarnished frames, and you have often wondered what they think of you: your father with his flat cap and his dead eyes, your mother at nineteen with the wind in her hair, Eli at seven missing a front tooth, Eli at seventeen already gone in the head though nobody knew it yet, and you between them, the one who stayed. She keeps them facing the door, so whoever comes in is looked at first. Sometimes, when the weather turns, she takes Eli's frame and wipes the glass with her apron, and she does not know she has done it ten thousand times.

Remember how it ended — you have it in you, every word, filed where the thunder can reach it. He had sold the boat, or meant to. He had his sea chest packed in the boathouse and his mind made up for the coast, and the farm, and her, and all of it was, to him, a trap with iron fittings. You said: "I hope the storm takes you." And he laughed — he actually laughed — and he went out of the house and down through the gate and into the black weather, and you stood at the window and watched him cross to the boathouse, and you did not follow to bring him back. You followed for another reason entirely, and you had that reason in your pocket all the while.

Much later — and it is later, it has always been later — the flare of a struck match finds nothing: oars, dust, the paper cities of wasps in the roof, a rope coiled like a sleeping animal, and no one. No one has ever been in there. Not tonight, not on any of the storm nights, not since the spring they dragged the beck and found his cap and never found him. You close the doors. You turn the brass key in the hasp until it clicks, because that is the part you have never been able to skip. It was you who shut that door — you with your shoulder against the planks while he beat on them and said your name, you who told yourself it was only to teach him, only for an hour, only until he promised. When the knocking stopped you unlocked it, and the room was empty, and the lake was wide, and you have been running the wrong way ever since. You walk home through the dying weather. Your mother is asleep in her chair with the lamp lit for you, and you hang the brass key on its nail in the porch, where it will wait, as you wait, for the next storm.
