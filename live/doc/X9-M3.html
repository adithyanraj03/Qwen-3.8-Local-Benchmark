Tat Tat

The brass key is warm in your fist, and the storm has already begun to knock at the door the way a stranger would, politely, without stopping. You live alone in the old keeper's cottage at the end of the headland, and you know the first hour of a storm the way you know your own hands: the hush, the drumming on the eaves, then the long moan that rolls down the cliff and around the house and settles, for a moment, in the walls. You take the key off the ring by the door, hold it through the first hour, and tell yourself the knocking is only the sea, arguing with the cliffs the way it has argued with them all along, and the way it will go on arguing, and the way it always has.

Her name was Elspeth, and she kept the light and the cottage and the headland for forty years, until the mainland came with its automatic lamps and its men in grey coats and the house went quiet. She told you all of this the summer you were old enough to be left through a night. She told you the cellar door was the only door in the house she would not open. And you never found your nerve to ask her why, and the summer ended the way summers end, and the train took you down the coast, and you did not write, and you did not go back. "There is no storm this house is afraid of. There is only weather that hasn't met the walls yet."

Under the eaves the storm gathers its voice, and you go through the cottage the way you have always gone through it when the sky turns. Kettle on the iron. Two chairs by the fire, one for you and one left empty, and you tell yourself the empty chair is for the night and not for the dead. You fill the deep blue tub, though the tap has never once in your life run dry. You hang the coats on their hooks, the heavy ones and the light ones, the way she did. You wipe the counter with the green cloth, the one that has lived in the drawer for longer than you have lived at all, and you put the teacups in the cabinet one at a time, handles to the left. You close the back door on the smell of the wet grass. And you wind the clock in the hall, the one that chimes a single minute and then stops, and has chimed that same minute, on and off, for as long as you can remember.

No one comes up the cliff path in a storm, so the knocking cannot be a visitor, and still your hands do the small fast trembling the old keepers called the weather shake. Outside the window the cliff goes black to black. There is no moon in it and no sky above it, only the sea flung sideways in long white streaks, and every so often a flash that lasts longer than lightning should, flat and yellow and patient, like a lamp burning in a window, like the lamp, like the one you are not supposed to keep watching, and you keep watching it all the same, because the dark on the cliff path is the only dark you have ever not been afraid of. It is the dark that takes the road down to the water, and the road is where she stood when the summer ended.

Doors. The house keeps so many of them, and tonight you keep meaning to lock them and keep not quite. The back door, which faces the sea. The door in the kitchen wall that Elspeth bricked up in her first winter and that no one remembers why. And the cellar door, with its round brass lock, the one the storm seems to find first and knock at most of all, the way weather finds a crack, the way a dog finds a bone, the way a hand finds the one thing it has been reaching for all along.

Every keeper since Elspeth kept the same brass key on the same ring by the door, and every keeper swore to the next that it was only for the cellar. You have never opened the cellar with it. You have held it in the firelight and read the teeth the way you would read a face, and it has the shape of something you have seen before, in a dream that is always almost finished when you wake. Tonight the key is warmer than the fire, warmer than the weather has any right to make it, and the knocking has changed. It is no longer at the door. It is at the cellar, and it is patient, and it is not going to stop.

Rattling, the shutters. By the second hour the roof begins to sing, a high wire sound under the wind, and the house leans, just barely, just enough for you to feel it in your teeth. The fire burns blue at the edges and will not warm the far corner of the room, and the clock in the hall chimes its minute and stops, and in the silence after the chiming you can hear it under the floor: the sea, far off, patient, in the cellar, though the cellar has never once in your life held anything but the dark. And under the sea, fainter than the sea, the sound of the house itself settling, creaking, the sound it made the first winter the door in the kitchen wall went silent under its brick.

Something you almost say is her name, and you stop yourself, because the name is yours to keep and the night is not your house to spend it in. But the empty chair is cold, and it is not cold, and you cross with the fire for the third time this hour, and you understand that you are not cross with the fire. You are cross with the hour. The kettle is at the boil for the third time, the same third time, and you set it down before it can scream, and the knocking goes on at the cellar, patient and polite, one, two, one, two, the tapping of fingers and not a fist.

Then the window goes out. Not dark: out, the way a picture goes out, so that where the sea was there is only a black so complete it has texture, and in the black the storm is at the cellar now, and below it, or inside it, a second sound you do not want to name, the sound of a person who has been walking somewhere for a very long time and has at last come to a door, and you say it anyway, to the glass, to the black, to the tapping. "It's only the hour. You're not the first."

Of all the things the storm has taken from the headland over the years, the post, the light, Elspeth's voice, the one thing it has never touched is the ring by the door, and on the ring there are two keys now. Yours. And the other, which is the shape of yours, which has the teeth you have read in the firelight all your life, and you know it with your whole quiet body before your mouth can say it. It is yours too.

Remembering is what the storm does to a house in the last hour. It takes the years, takes the weather, takes the summer you were nine and the summer you were fifteen, and leaves the shapes of them in the walls, so that you can stand in the kitchen and feel, without seeing, the exact spot where Elspeth stood when she told you the cellar door was the only door in the world that was ever locked from the outside. It leaves the feel of her hands on the kettle, the weight of them, the patience of them, and you stand in the kitchen and let the house remember for you, because your own remembering is not enough, not for a night like this, not for a morning that will not come.

Morning never comes to the headland, and that is how you know the brass key was never for the cellar. It is the key to the hour, and you have been turning it all your life, one click, one click, keeping the storm at the first hour, keeping the sea at the foot of the cliff, keeping the morning of 1903, the morning the sky went out and left only the knocking, a minute from arriving. The tapping stops, and you understand at last what the tapping was. It was not the storm, asking in. It was your own hand on the door of the next minute, knocking for itself to come. "Come in. It's only the hour." The fire holds its breath. The empty chair is not for the dead. It is for the morning, and you set the key down on the ring, and you let the hour turn.
