Live Not on Evil

The storm crossed the county line an hour before you did, and you drove into it on purpose. A person should not be seen grieving at a grave they did not attend, and you meant to be seen by no one after twenty-two years on the lane to the house of the man who broke your mother. The town had folded itself under a black lid since noon, and now the downpour came apart over your windshield like gravel. In your pocket your thumb worried the brass key you had lifted out of the dead man's watch pocket at the funeral home while nobody was looking. He had kept it through the hospital, through the last winter when the creek took his boots and half his porch. You had come for whatever he kept of yours, and you had brought a tire iron in case love failed you.

Hail chased your high beams down the lane in silver fistfuls, and the wheat lay flat against the fence wire like something just struck. You used to count the fence posts on the bicycle to school, all two hundred and six, all the way to his gate, and then you spent twenty-two years becoming a careful stranger. He sounded impossibly old at Christmas. He said your name once. You said maybe. Neither of you mentioned next again. He died in February with the creek over its banks. You did not go. Your sister went, and has not forgiven you, and you cannot tell her you have not forgiven yourself either.

Under the porch eaves the storm gave you quarter, and still the water found your collar and ran down your spine like a cold hand taking its time. The one yellow window in the whole house was his study. You stood on the wet boards until your breathing behaved, and you went over the plan: the tin box he had palmed at your mother's will reading while her lawyer was still uncapping his pen; the deed he swore was inside it; the short laugh that came after, as if the roof over your head had been a thing to win at cards. The wind shoved the whole length of the roof. You reached for the knob, and the front door opened before you touched it, so old and oiled, like a mouth kept open for words it had waited years to say.

No one knew you were there, and at eight your sister called anyway, because that is the law between sisters. You took the call on the porch with the storm working at the shingles. "Don't go into that house, Mara. Let it rot, and let him rot." She had stayed; she had earned verbs like that. You looked at the yellow window, at the doorframe where you used to hang wreaths, said what you always said to her, nearly nothing, and hung up gently. Then you went in, and the hall took you the way deep water takes a stone, without argument, all at once.

Downstairs it smelled of kerosene, pipe smoke, and the wet wool of his good coat on its hook, as if he had only stepped out into this exact storm. The deluge leaned on the west wall until the frame creaked out a question you would not answer. You found the study by heart, past the bathroom with its cracked blue tile, past the bedroom where your mother had kept his side made for thirty years. On the desk, under the green-shaded lamp that had been burning for whoever comes home, sat the tin box. Locked, of course. Everything he had ever loved, he put a lock on.

Everything about that little box was an insult. You set it under the lamp and turned the brass key across your knuckles: heavy old work, teeth cut long, bow filed down and worn smooth as a worry stone. He had cut a key for every lock in this house except the one door he never shut. You fitted it to the hasp and it came open like a promise kept, and inside was not the deed. A child's blue enamel barrette. A tin soldier with his paint gone. A county library card, one name, M. ABBOTT, age seven. Your small things, boxed and kept like relics of a saint who left no forwarding address.

Rows of envelopes lay beneath, second and third deep, yours in your bad schoolgirl scrawl, every furious letter you ever mailed him, and he had answered all of them, forty letters in his mason's block capitals on paper from the hardware store, forty times saying the same three things, no sermon and no score kept: glad you wrote, door is open, dinner is whenever you want it. You sat in his chair, and it creaked your old weight back at you, and you read a decade of an old man learning to say almost nothing and mean all of it while the storm threw itself at the glass.

Somebody should have told you that a man can lock love up and call it keeping. You carried the box to the armchair and sat through your sister's second call going to voicemail, and while the storm got tired you held the old twenty-two-year story up against the evidence, and it did not fit. So you went looking. In the desk you found a tractor receipt, a mortgage paid in full the year after your parents split, and a register of checks, twelve hundred dollars every December beginning the winter you left, made out to a trust with your name in it, each returned by the bank unclaimed, none by you. Your mother, who burned his letters in the yard for you to watch, had told you on her last good day that he left you nothing, and had been believed.

The last envelope lay loose in the drawer, marked in his shaking capitals FOR MARA WHENEVER, and you read it in the lamplight. The date was eight days before the creek. He wrote down the drinking, the shouting, the broken plate, the night you left, the whole ledger of the man you thought he was. He wrote that he went up to the barn next morning with a ladder and found only the open window, the tire iron gone from his truck, and a smear of your blood on the rail where you had thrown it over your shoulder climbing down at seventeen. He wrote that he never once believed you stayed away for want of love, and that he had hoped the weather would drive you home before he ran out of years. Under that he had begun a sentence the pen never finished, running off into the blank part of the page, into the February that came after.

Outside, the storm let go of the house around four, the way tired men give up. You sat on the back steps and watched the creek try the porch posts and miss. The tire iron was still in your coat pocket, where you had carried it up from the truck and set down without ever deciding to, and you let it stay. You thought about the barn, and the plate, and how you had crossed two thousand miles to break into a house and had instead sat up all night reading a dead man's forty letters. The sky went gray, then sorry white, and you said aloud, to nobody, to the porch, to him: "I'm home."

Rest came without asking, in the room at the end of the hall you had not dared to look into. The door stood open. The walls were the blue your mother painted, and in the window glass your seven-year-old initials still caught the gray morning, scratched with a nail. The bed was made, and the dust lay over it like a verdict no one would lift. At nine your sister called with the will: the farm, the house, all of it to Mara; and then, trying not to cry, "He wouldn't let them sell the timber, kept telling the lawyer it was yours." And on the flood night he had gone down into the water moving the last of your boxes upstairs, and that was where they found him in the morning, holding a crate stenciled with your name the way a man holds beads.

Morning came up clean over a drowned county, and you stood in your own kitchen and said your own kitchen aloud. The words fit, which nothing had done for twenty-two years, and you turned the key over in the new sun, read what a lifetime of thumbs had rubbed bright in the brass, M.A., scratched by the same nail that had signed the window glass, at seven. To it hung the paper tag he had kept, his grown-up writing on it: M.'s key, found in the barn, August the ninth, bring it home someday. He had carried it through the hospital, the flood, into the ground itself, keeping it for you to the last hour, and you had pried it out of his coat and called it stolen. In the margin of his last letter he had written his own father's motto, live not on evil. You hung the key on the hook behind your door, over the floor you learned to walk on, and made coffee for one in the house you came home to. The storm went off over the next county, and you let it go.
